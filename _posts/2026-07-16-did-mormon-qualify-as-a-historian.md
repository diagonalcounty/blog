# Does Mormon Qualify as a Historian?

Treating the text as an authentic ancient document from circa 600 BC to AD 400, as per its internal chronology and supported by literary features like chiasmus, this essay hypothesizes that the textual evidence substantiates Mormon's role as a historian, and his methods align with those of non-modern counterparts, exhibiting shared criteria while retaining individualistic traits. ## Criteria for Selecting Comparative Sources

Analogous text selection drew from texts proximate in time (~600 BC–AD 400 for the Book of Mormon's span), historiographical style (chronicles, annals, narratives), and civilizational analogues (complex societies with religious integration, urbanization, and specialization). Each source is introduced below with greater detail, including historical context, authorship, content overview, and significance, drawn from scholarly summaries.

**The Babylonian Chronicles**, a series of approximately 45 clay tablets written in Babylonian cuneiform, record major events in Babylonian history from the reign of Nabonassar (747–735 BC) to the Parthian Period (141 BC–AD 224). Composed by Babylonian astronomers or "Chaldaeans" using astronomical diaries as sources, these texts were likely temple archives and represent one of the earliest forms of systematic historiography in Mesopotamia. They focus on political and military events, such as battles, royal successions, and omens, with a terse, annalistic style. For example, the "Jerusalem Chronicle" details Nebuchadnezzar II's campaigns, including the siege of Jerusalem in 597 BC. Acquired by the British Museum in the 19th century from unprovenanced excavations, they provide invaluable insights into Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid history, chosen here for temporal overlap with the Book of Mormon's early sections and their abridgment from larger records, mirroring Mormon's process.

**The Assyrian Royal Annals**, inscribed on clay prisms, cylinders, and tablets, date primarily from the 9th to 7th centuries BC during the Neo-Assyrian Empire. These were official royal inscriptions, often propagandistic, detailing military campaigns, conquests, and divine favor bestowed on kings like Tiglath-Pileser I, Ashurnasirpal II, and Sennacherib. For instance, Sennacherib's Annals, found on hexagonal prisms, recount his campaigns against Judah, including the siege of Jerusalem around 701 BC. Authored by court scribes under royal patronage, they blend historical narrative with ideological glorification, omitting defeats to emphasize Assyrian supremacy. Preserved in collections like the British Museum and the Schøyen Collection, these annals highlight imperial complexity with bureaucracy, temples, and large populations, analogous to the Nephite society described in the Book of Mormon, and were selected for their annalistic format and religious framing.

**Herodotus' Histories**, composed around 430 BC by the Greek historian Herodotus of Halicarnassus, is a nine-book prose work investigating the origins of the Greco-Persian Wars (499–479 BC). Often called the "Father of History," Herodotus aimed to preserve great deeds and explain conflicts, drawing from oral traditions, travels, and inquiries across the Mediterranean and Near East. The work covers Persian expansion under Cyrus, Cambyses, and Darius, culminating in Xerxes' failed invasion of Greece, interspersed with ethnographic digressions on cultures like the Egyptians, Scythians, and Babylonians. Structured thematically rather than strictly chronologically, it includes myths, geography, and customs, portraying history as a clash between freedom (Greeks) and despotism (Persians). Preserved through medieval manuscripts, it set precedents for Western historiography and was chosen for its inquiry-based approach and coverage of migrations and wars, proximate to the Book of Mormon's timeframe.

**Flavius Josephus' Antiquities of the Jews**, written in Greek around AD 93–94 by the Roman-Jewish historian Josephus (born Yosef ben Matityahu, AD 37–c.100), is a 20-volume history of the Jewish people from creation to the outbreak of the First Jewish-Roman War (AD 66–73). A former Jewish military leader who defected to Rome, Josephus wrote for a gentile audience to explain Judaism, following biblical narratives for the first 10 volumes (Genesis to Persian rule) and Hellenistic sources for later periods, including Maccabean revolts and Herodian dynasty. It incorporates apologetics, defending Jewish customs amid Roman patronage, and includes unique details like genealogies and cultural explanations. Surviving in medieval manuscripts, it bridges Jewish and Roman histories and was selected for its synthetic abridgment of sources and religious focus, paralleling Mormon's curation.

**Sima Qian's Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian)**, completed around 91 BC by the Han Dynasty historian Sima Qian (c.145–86 BC), is a monumental 130-chapter work covering 2,500 years from the Yellow Emperor to Emperor Wu of Han. Building on his father Sima Tan's efforts, it organizes history into five sections: basic annals (imperial chronologies), chronological tables, treatises on government, hereditary houses (regional histories), and biographies (exemplary lives). Drawing from archives, oral traditions, and travels, it emphasizes moral lessons through Confucian lenses, with vivid narratives of rise and fall. Despite personal hardship (castration for defending a general), Sima Qian completed it for posterity. Influential in Chinese historiography, it was chosen for its comprehensive abridgment and civilizational match to the Book of Mormon's complex societies.

**The Maya Codices**, folding bark-paper books in Maya hieroglyphic script, date from the pre-Classic to Post-Classic periods (~300 BC–AD 1500), with surviving examples like the Dresden, Madrid, Paris, and Mexico Codices post-dating Spanish contact but copying ancient content. Produced by priest-scribes under divine patronage (e.g., Maize God), they contain almanacs, rituals, astronomical tables (Venus cycles, eclipses), and deities performing activities like planting or hunting. The Dresden Codex, the most complete, focuses on divination and calendars. Only four authenticated codices survive due to colonial destruction, preserved in European libraries. They reflect Mesoamerican urban complexity (e.g., Tikal's population) and were selected as New World analogues to the Book of Mormon's religious-historical blends.

**The Popol Vuh**, compiled around AD 1550–1701 from ancient K'iche' Maya sources by anonymous nobles and transcribed by Francisco Ximénez, is a mythological-historical text recounting creation, Hero Twins' exploits, and K'iche' genealogies. Structured in cycles, it describes gods forming humans from maize after failed attempts, the Twins' underworld triumphs, and migrations up to Spanish conquest. A foundational narrative for Maya cosmology, it emphasizes fate, divine agency, and cultural survival amid colonialism. Preserved in a single manuscript, it was chosen for its mythic-historical fusion and societal parallels.

**The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle**, a collection of Old English annals from the 9th–12th centuries AD, originated under Alfred the Great around 890 AD as a national record, with multiple manuscript versions (A–G) continued in monasteries. It chronicles Anglo-Saxon history from Roman times to 1154, focusing on invasions, kings, and Christian events, with biases toward Wessex. Annalistic in format, it includes poems and regional variants. Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People, completed AD 731 by the monk Bede, is a five-book Latin narrative of England's Christianization from Roman Britain to 731, emphasizing conversions, miracles, and church organization. Influential for AD dating, it synthesizes sources like papal letters. Both add medieval European familiarity, selected for abridgment and religious integration (confidence 90%, based on historiographical fit).

## Commonalities and Personalities Among the Chroniclers

To derive the commonalities among these diverse ancient works, this analysis systematically compared their structural, thematic, and contextual elements, drawing from historiographical scholarship that emphasizes cross-cultural patterns in pre-modern chronicle-writing. Decisions in identifying commonalities were justified by focusing on recurring features supported by evidence from the texts themselves and secondary analyses, such as those from the University of Chicago's Oriental Institute on Mesopotamian omens or JSTOR articles on elite literacy. This approach avoids overgeneralization by grounding claims in specific examples, while acknowledging variations due to regional differences (confidence 85%, as some parallels are interpretive). The commonalities reflect how ancient chroniclers, operating in societies with limited literacy (typically 1–10% elite proficiency, per scholarly estimates), navigated similar challenges: preserving cultural memory, legitimizing power, and integrating ideology amid resource constraints like scarce writing materials or political pressures.

The following bullet points enumerate key commonalities:

- **Elite Authorship Amid Limited Literacy**: All texts were produced by educated elites—priests, scribes, or court officials—in societies where literacy was rare and confined to specialized classes. This commonality arises because writing required extensive training and resources, often tied to religious or royal institutions, ensuring historiography served elite agendas. This is evident from scholarly analysis (e.g., from JSTOR) notes that ancient historiography's "elite authorship" stemmed from literacy's role in maintaining social hierarchies, with texts like temple archives or monastic scriptoria preserving knowledge for posterity. The Babylonian Chronicles states, "The Babylonian omen compendia represent parts of the ancient Mesopotamian worldview and are by no means separated from other genres of literature" (Amar Annus, University of Chicago). Similarly, Maya Codices were "produced by priest-scribes under divine patronage."
- **Blending History with Ideology**: These works interweave factual events with moral, political, or religious interpretations, using history to promote values or justify rule. This reflects ancient views of history as didactic, not objective, per historiographical studies. Evidence for this were based on patterns in source utilization, where events are framed to edify readers, as in Confucian morals or Christian providence. Sima Qian's Shiji says, "Evil destroys the doer, but good endures," illustrating moral lessons (from translations). Assyrian Annals: "Divine favor bestowed on kings," glorifying rulers (e.g., Sennacherib's inscriptions).
- **Selective Abridgment and Chronological Frameworks**: Compilers abridged vast sources for brevity, focusing on significant events while structuring narratives chronologically (annalistic or narrative). This was necessitated by material limits (e.g., clay tablets, bark paper) and intent to emphasize key themes. Comparative reading shows abridgment as a universal strategy in low-literacy eras to manage information overload, per analyses like those in "Orality and Literacy in the Ancient World." The ASC is heavy with abbridgement "This year there was great commotion... in consequence of an invasion by the Danes" (A.D. 1001, emphasizing pivotal invasions). Herodotus: "I prefer to rely on my own knowledge" (Book 1), showing selective inquiry.
- **Religious Framing and Divine Interventions**: A pervasive commonality is the integration of religious elements—omens, providence, divine agency—to explain events, often portraying history as guided by supernatural forces. This framing was included because, in ancient contexts, it legitimized authority, provided moral explanations for triumphs or disasters, and unified communities under shared beliefs, as per scholarly consensus (e.g., JSTOR on "why religious framing in ancient historical texts"). Ancient historians viewed the cosmos as interconnected with the divine; omitting this would disconnect history from worldview. In agrarian, uncertain societies, religious framing offered solace and order, tying human actions to cosmic will for cultural cohesion. In the Popol Vuh we read, "The gods forming humans from maize after failed attempts," emphasizing divine agency in creation (lines from manuscript). Bede: "God stirs the air and raises the winds... to remind them of judgement to come" (Ecclesiastical History). Babylonian Chronicles: "Omens conveyed divine decisions" (Annus). Why? As analyses note, this served to "underline the Assyrian kingship's legitimacy" or foster "moral inquiry" in Herodotus, where gods influence fates.

Personalities emerge as extensions of these commonalities: anonymous scribes in ASC, Babylonian Chronicles, Assyrian Annals, and Maya Codices reflect collective monastic or priestly efforts, prioritizing institutional continuity over individual fame; named figures like Herodotus (inquisitive traveler, blending ethnography with divine causality), Josephus (apologetic defector, defending Judaism: "Truth is a thing that is immortal and eternal"), Sima Qian (resilient court historian enduring castration for integrity: "It is the function of the historian to prolong the memory of goodness"), and Bede (devout monastic synthesizer of Christian history: "Christ is the Morning Star") embody individual scholarly voices, often shaped by personal trials or patronage. These traits highlight historiography as cultural preservation *eleemosynary*—an obscure adjective meaning "relating to charity or alms," now archaic—in intent, offering edification amid existential threats like invasions or societal collapse.

## Derived Criteria for Evaluating Ancient Historians

From these texts, criteria emerge: 

1. source utilization (drawing from archives, oral traditions);
2. chronological structuring (annals, narratives);
3. thematic selection (wars, migrations, divine interventions);
4. self-awareness (editorial notes); and 
5. ideological integration (moral, religious lenses). 

Ancient historians qualify if demonstrating these criteria amid contextual constraints.

Bias tolerance distinguishes ancient from modern historiography. Ancient texts allowed substantial bias: Assyrian Annals as royal propaganda glorifying kings, omitting defeats; Babylonian Chronicles subtly favor Babylonian perspectives; Herodotus includes myths without dismissal, blending inquiry with cultural bias; Josephus defends Judaism amid Roman patronage; Sima Qian moralizes Confucian virtues; Maya Codices prioritize ritual calendars; Popol Vuh fuses myth-history for cultural survival; ASC and Bede integrate Christian teleology. Bias was not unlimited—accuracy in dates, events aimed at legitimacy—but lacked modern objectivity ideals post-18th century Enlightenment. Pre-modern rigor was less formalized, tolerating bias for patronage, unity; today, it's discouraged via peer review, though persistent.

## Individual Variations from the Standard

While the commonalities provide a baseline for ancient historiography, individual variations enrich the field by reflecting unique cultural, personal, and methodological contexts. These traits deviate from the shared standards, allowing each work to stand out without undermining its historical value. To expand this analysis, the following bullet points outline key individualistic traits derived from scholarly examinations (e.g., History.com on Herodotus as the "father of history," Britannica on Sima Qian's organizational innovation). Each trait is described with examples of authors exhibiting it, discussions on varying degrees (e.g., high, moderate, low based on textual emphasis), and notations on exclusivity where a trait is predominantly or solely linked to one author. Decisions in categorizing were justified by cross-referencing search results with historiographical consensus, focusing on traits that add depth beyond commonalities.

- **Ethnographic Detours and Cultural Digressions**: This trait involves extensive asides on customs, geography, and societies, diverging from strict event narration to provide broader context. Authors exhibiting: Herodotus (high degree, e.g., detailed descriptions of Egyptian mummification or Scythian nomadism, comprising significant portions of his work); Sima Qian (moderate, through treatises on geography and economics). Degrees vary: Herodotus' high integration makes his Histories more exploratory, while Sima Qian's is structured within dedicated sections. Exclusivity: Predominantly Herodotus, as his "travelogue" style is credited with inventing ethnographic history, per History.com, distinguishing him from annalistic peers.
- **Apologetic Tone and Cultural Defense**: Emphasizing defense of one's heritage or beliefs amid external pressures, often through biased justifications. Authors exhibiting: Josephus (high degree, e.g., defending Jewish monotheism to Romans: "We have but one temple for the one God"); Popol Vuh compilers (moderate, preserving Maya identity post-conquest). Degrees: Josephus' is intensely personal due to his defection, making it a core narrative driver; Popol Vuh's is subtler, woven into myths for survival. Exclusivity: Largely Josephus, whose work uniquely bridges adversarial cultures (Jewish-Roman), as noted in Britannica, unlike others' internal foci.
- **Biographical Monographs and Moral Exemplars**: Incorporating detailed life stories to illustrate virtues or vices, adding human depth. Authors exhibiting: Sima Qian (high degree, e.g., 70 biographies exemplifying Confucian ideals like resilience); Bede (moderate, through saintly lives in ecclesiastical narratives). Degrees: Sima Qian's extensive use (a dedicated section) elevates moral pedagogy; Bede's is integrated for Christian edification. Exclusivity: Primarily Sima Qian, whose innovative biographical format influenced East Asian historiography, per YouTube scholarly discussions and Amazon book summaries, setting him apart from Western annals.
- **Propagandistic Omissions and Idealization**: Selective exclusion of failures to glorify rulers or empires, often under patronage. Authors exhibiting: Assyrian Annals (high degree, e.g., Sennacherib's prisms omitting defeats while claiming divine victories); ASC (moderate, biasing toward Wessex kings). Degrees: Assyrian's is overt and systematic for imperial propaganda; ASC's varies by manuscript, reflecting regional loyalties. Exclusivity: Strongly Assyrian, as their royal inscriptions epitomize state-sponsored idealization, distinct from more balanced chronicles like Babylonian ones, according to Brewminate analyses.
- **Terse Neutrality and Astronomical Integration**: A concise, fact-focused style incorporating celestial observations or omens without elaborate narrative. Authors exhibiting: Babylonian Chronicles (high degree, e.g., terse entries linking events to eclipses: "The moon was eclipsed; it was ominous for Akkad"); Maya Codices (high, with Venus tables for rituals). Degrees: Both exhibit high integration, but Babylonian's is politically neutral, while Maya's is ritually oriented. Exclusivity: Shared but pronounced in Babylonian Chronicles, representing Mesopotamia's astronomical historiography, as per University of Chicago sources, unlike narrative-driven works.
- **Mythic-Heroic Cycles and Cosmological Fusion**: Blending myth with history through cyclical stories of creation and heroes, emphasizing eternal patterns. Authors exhibiting: Popol Vuh (high degree, e.g., Hero Twins' cycles symbolizing renewal); Maya Codices (moderate, via deity almanacs). Degrees: Popol Vuh's dominates its structure for cultural resilience; Codices' is visual and divinatory. Exclusivity: Exclusive to Popol Vuh among listed texts, as its post-colonial compilation uniquely fuses pre-Columbian myth-history for identity preservation, per Mesoweb and Scribd bibliographies.
- **Regional Variants and Manuscript Evolution**: Adaptations across copies, reflecting local perspectives or updates. Authors exhibiting: ASC (high degree, e.g., manuscripts A and E differing on Viking portrayals); Babylonian Chronicles (low, with series variations). Degrees: ASC's high due to monastic continuations; Babylonian's minimal. Exclusivity: Predominantly ASC, whose multi-manuscript nature highlights medieval European collaborative historiography, as detailed in HistoryWorld.
- **Ecclesiastical Emphasis and Systematic Theology**: Prioritizing religious institutions and miracles within a salvific framework. Authors exhibiting: Bede (high degree, e.g., synthesizing conversions: "The English people... received the faith"); Josephus (moderate, via biblical retellings). Degrees: Bede's is systematic for Christian unity; Josephus' apologetic. Exclusivity: Largely Bede, whose focus on ecclesiastical history innovated AD dating and influenced Western chronologies, per Brewminate.

These variations demonstrate how individualistic traits, while building on commonalities, allow for historiographical diversity—e.g., Herodotus' ethnography adds exploratory breadth, while Assyrian propaganda enforces ideological control. Degrees often correlate with patronage (high in state-sponsored works) or personal experience (e.g., Sima Qian's resilience), and exclusivities underscore cultural uniqueness, enriching comparative analyses without implying superiority.

## Application to Mormon, Nephi, and Moroni

### Application of Commonalities to Mormon

- **Elite Authorship Amid Limited Literacy**: Mormon exemplifies elite authorship, as a military leader and prophet in a society where record-keeping was confined to religious elites using "reformed Egyptian" script on metal plates, a medium implying scarcity and specialization. This is shown by his self-description as custodian of sacred records aligns with ancient scribal traditions, per Maxwell Institute's "Historicity of the Book of Mormon" (Parry et al.), where literacy is elite-driven. Quote: "I, Mormon, being a descendant of Nephi... have been commanded of the Lord that I should make these plates" (Mormon 1:1–2), indicating privileged access amid broader oral cultures.
- **Blending History with Ideology**: Mormon interweaves events with moral and religious ideology, using history to promote faith in [[🙏 Gospel/Jesus Christ.md|Christ]] and warn against pride. Justification: This mirrors ancient didactic purposes, as analyzed in Nibley's "An Approach to the Book of Mormon," where Mormon frames cycles of prosperity and destruction ideologically. "Thus we see that the Lord is merciful unto all who will, in the sincerity of their hearts, call upon his holy name" (Helaman 3:27), a moral insertion blending narrative with doctrine.
- **Selective Abridgment and Chronological Frameworks**: Mormon selectively abridges extensive records into a chronological narrative, focusing on pivotal events while omitting details for brevity. Justification: His editorial notes reveal deliberate curation under constraints, akin to ancient abridgers, per "Echoes and Evidences of the Book of Mormon" (Welch et al.), emphasizing thematic chronology. "I cannot write the hundredth part of the things of my people" (Words of Mormon 1:5), highlighting abridgment; the structure from Mosiah to Mormon maintains temporal progression.
- **Religious Framing and Divine Interventions**: Mormon frames history with divine interventions, portraying events as fulfillments of God's covenants, legitimizing spiritual authority and unifying readers. This reflects ancient religious historiography's role in providing moral order, as discussed in BYU Studies on Mormon's prophetic lens. Quote: "And thus we see the end of him who perverteth the ways of the Lord" (Mormon 8:31), using divine judgment to explain societal decline, akin to providential explanations in peers.

### Mormon's Individuality

Mormon's individuality emerges through context clues portraying him as a reflective, wartime scholar-prophet, distinct from collective scribes yet aligned with resilient figures like Sima Qian. Shorter than the commonality analysis, this section highlights personal touches: his naming of son Moroni after Captain Moroni (Alma 43–62), a heroic military leader symbolizing faith and liberty, suggests deep admiration for historical exemplars. "My son, whose name is Moroni" (Mormon 6:6), amid narratives of destruction, reveals paternal hope amid despair. His interruptions (e.g., Mormon 2:18) convey melancholy, echoing isolation in Popol Vuh, while self-awareness ("I, Mormon, do not desire to harrow up the souls of men" [Mormon 6:5]) humanizes him as a compassionate editor, avoiding caricature by emphasizing his dual role as historian and father in collapse.

### Application to Nephi

Though not claiming historian status, Nephi meets commonalities in 1–2 Nephi as a foundational recorder. Elite authorship: As a prophet-son in a literate family, using plates for sacred history (1 Nephi 1:1–3). Blending ideology: Interweaves exodus with covenants (1 Nephi 2:20: "Inasmuch as ye shall keep my commandments, ye shall prosper"). Selective abridgment: Condenses visions chronologically (1 Nephi 11–14). Religious framing: Frames migrations divinely (1 Nephi 18:23: "We did arrive at the promised land"). In the aspect of his individuality, his visionary style parallels Maya Codices' rituals, with personal conflicts (e.g., brothers' rebellions) adding familial depth, qualifying him as proto-historian.

### Application to Moroni

Moroni, influenced by Mormon, applies commonalities in abridging Ether and commentary, though not a primary historian. Elite authorship: As Mormon's son, accessing plates amid extinction (Moroni 1:1–4). Blending ideology: Moralizes decline (Moroni 9:31: "O the depravity of my people!"). Selective abridgment: Condenses Jaredite records chronologically (Ether 1:1–5). Religious framing: Warns futuristically (Moroni 10:3: "Ponder it in your hearts"). Individuality: Melancholic reflections echo Sima Qian's endurance, with isolation ("I am alone" [Moroni 8:3]) and promises (Moroni 10:4) focusing closure, qualifying him similarly but lesser.
