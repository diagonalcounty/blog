Research note on the word *salvation*—English history, comparative frameworks, and especially King Benjamin’s usage in Mosiah 4:8 (covenant, retention, charity). Compiled from multi-source notes; interpretive claims about “ancient” parallels are reconstructive.

## English etymology

When “salvation” first entered English, its meaning was almost exclusively religious. English already had the Germanic word “rescue” for physical saving, so “salvation” was adopted specifically for the Christian concept of being saved from hell or sin.

Linguists trace Latin *salvus* to a Proto-Indo-European root *solh₂-* (variants *sol-*), core sense “whole,” “well-kept,” or “intact.” Reflexes appear in classical Latin. The word largely displaced native Old English *hǣlu* (cognate with “health” and “whole”), which had covered overlapping ideas of safety and well-being.

Earliest firm English attestation: early 13th century (OED evidence ca. 1225 in the *Ancrene Wisse*). Trajectory reflects heavy medieval Christian Latin influence on English religious vocabulary. Cognates/related terms: *save*, *safe*, *salve*, *salubrious*, *salvage*.

## Comparative religious frameworks

### Abrahamic traditions

- **Christianity** — Rescue from sin and its consequences through Jesus Christ’s atonement, received via faith, grace, and/or works (varies by denomination). Early NT Greek *sōtēria* (noun) and *sōzō* (verb) had a broad Koine range: rescue from physical peril, healing, preservation of well-being or safe return, deliverance from demonic forces or sin’s effects, restoration to wholeness. Everyday papyri and classical Greek often defaulted to bodily health/wellness. Over centuries, especially in Western theology, emphasis specialized toward spiritual and eschatological dimensions (justification, sanctification, eternal life). The name *Yeshua* (Hebrew form underlying “Jesus”) derives from the same root as “salvation”—“He saves” or “YHWH is salvation.”

- **Judaism** — Hebrew root *yashaʿ* (ישע) / *yeshaʿ*, yielding *yeshuʿah* (“salvation,” “deliverance,” “help,” “victory”). Usages often stress concrete rescue from enemies, oppression, or trouble (Exodus as archetype), plus moral/repentant and future-oriented hopes (world to come, messianic deliverance). Emphasis tends to be more collective and covenantal—fidelity to Torah and Israel’s destiny—than highly individualized “personal salvation from sin,” though personal righteousness remains essential. *Yeshua* (Joshua/Jesus) carries the same root meaning.

- **Islam** — Eventual entrance to Paradise (*Jannah*) and avoidance of Hell through sincere faith in the one God, submission (*islām*), righteous deeds, the Five Pillars, and divine mercy. Works and faith interact; ultimate reliance rests on God’s compassion rather than human merit alone.

### Indian and related traditions

These more typically frame analogous ideas as self-realized liberation rather than rescue by an external agent:

- **Hinduism** — *moksha* (liberation) from *samsara* (cycle of rebirth and suffering) and realization of unity with or identity in Brahman. Paths include knowledge (*jnana*), devotion (*bhakti*), action (*karma*), and meditation/ritual (varies by school).
- **Buddhism** — *nirvana*—extinguishing craving, ignorance, and suffering—and release from *samsara*, via the Four Noble Truths and Noble Eightfold Path. Some Mahayana traditions incorporate aid from bodhisattvas.
- **Jainism** — *moksha* via ethical purification, non-violence, and right faith/knowledge/conduct.

Many other traditions express parallel concepts around cosmic harmony, ancestral relations, or escape from malevolent forces. Significant internal variation exists within each tradition.

### Secular, metaphorical, and philosophical extensions

Outside religion, “salvation” denotes any rescue, preservation, or deliverance from peril, failure, or difficulty (e.g. “policies offered salvation for the industry”; “music was his salvation from despair”). Philosophically it can evoke liberation from illusion, ignorance, or existential constraint, echoing certain Eastern or classical motifs.

## King Benjamin and Mosiah 4:8

Analyzing Benjamin’s use of “salvation” requires looking past English dictionaries to hypothesized Semitic roots and the rhetorical setting of his address. His vocabulary points to a covenantal, communal, and transformational view of salvation—distinct from modern, individualized Western theology.

### Setting and the verse

> “And this is the means whereby salvation cometh. And there is none other salvation save this which hath been spoken of; neither are there any conditions whereby man can be saved except the conditions which I have told you.” (Mosiah 4:8)

The verse sits in the third, practical section of Benjamin’s oration (Mosiah 2–5), delivered from a tower at the temple during a covenant-renewal assembly and the coronation of his son Mosiah. The people have already been spiritually awakened, fallen to the earth, and cried for mercy through the atoning blood of Christ (Mosiah 4:1–3). Benjamin then lays out the “means” (the atonement prepared from the foundation of the world) and insists salvation is exclusive and conditional on what he has taught: believing in God’s existence, creative power, wisdom, and incomprehensibility; repenting and forsaking sins; humbling oneself; sincerely asking forgiveness—with the charge that belief must issue in action (“if you believe all these things see that ye do them,” v. 10).

Later in the same chapter he adds ongoing retention of a remission of sins “from day to day” through remembering dependence on God, steadfast faith, and—strikingly—imparting substance to the poor (feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, visiting the sick) as concrete expression of that faith (vv. 11–12, 26). The chapter closes with a call to watch thoughts, words, and deeds and continue in faith “even unto the end of your lives” lest one perish (v. 30).

Research on the structure of Mosiah 2–5 places the speech in the frame of an ancient Israelite-style coronation and covenant-renewal festival, often compared to the autumn Feast of Tabernacles (*Sukkot*).

### Semitic roots in the speech’s lexicon

The claimed original language (reformed Egyptian with underlying Hebrew influences) is described as lost; analysis works with the received English text and literary features scholars identify in it.

- **Yasha (deliverance / space)** — Primary Hebrew root for salvation (*yasha*, forming words like *yeshua*). Physical connotation: being brought into a wide, spacious place, free from distress, confinement, or siege. Active deliverance, not a passive static state. (Full constellation below.)
- **Root of “Mosiah”** — Benjamin’s son and the book’s name derive from Hebrew *m-s-h* (or *m-w-s-y-h*), “savior” / “deliverer.” Discourse on salvation is tied to the deliverance theme of his dynasty and the book.

### Covenantal and communal character

Modern contexts often frame salvation as a private transaction. Benjamin’s use is thoroughly covenantal:

- **Royal adoption** — Salvation is the process of being adopted into a new royal lineage. Those who enter the covenant become “the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters” (Mosiah 5:7).
- **Taking a name** — Salvation is synonymous with taking upon oneself a specific name: formally entering a covenant community that bears the name of the sovereign.

### Conditions of salvation (Mosiah 4:8)

Benjamin says there are no conditions whereby man can be saved “except the conditions which I have told you.” In context those conditions include:

- **Ontological transformation** — Baseline human condition is the “natural man,” enemy to God (Mosiah 3:19). Salvation is not merely a change in legal standing (being forgiven); it requires “putting off” the natural state and changing disposition to become a “saint.”
- **Acknowledgment of “nothingness”** — Stark economic and spatial language: “nothingness” of man, “unprofitable servants.” Salvation requires awakening to complete dependence on God (Mosiah 4:5, 11).
- **Social justice as saving principle** — Immediately after 4:8, retention of salvation is linked to communal obligation: because all are “beggars” before God, maintaining a remission of sins requires imparting substance to the poor (Mosiah 4:16–26). Salvation is verified by how a person treats the marginalized.

### Rhetorical structure and parallelism

Mosiah 4:8 uses synonymous Hebraic parallelism:

> *“And there is **none other salvation** save this which hath been spoken of;*  
> *neither are there **any conditions** whereby man can be saved except the conditions which I have told you.”*

Duplicating the thought (none other salvation / no other conditions) seals loopholes and emphasizes the exclusivity of the Atonement as the sole mechanism for deliverance.

### Distinctive features of Benjamin’s voice

Within the Book of Mormon’s presentation, Benjamin is a distinctive speaker. He taught his sons “all the language of his fathers” (Hebrew and Egyptian, Mosiah 1:2–4). His oration is often analyzed as a unified address marked by parallelism, chiasmus, careful repetition, conditional structures, and a blend of doctrinal depth with ethical application. Studies in the Welch and Ricks collection *King Benjamin’s Speech: “That Ye May Learn Wisdom”* identify a recognizable register: authoritative yet self-effacing, covenantal, hortatory, and relational. Later Nephite voices quote or allude to his formulations.

Further dimensions from placement in Benjamin’s mouth:

- **Exclusivity tied to enumerated, actionable conditions** — Not only “through Christ,” but “none other salvation save this,” anchored to the conditions *he has just delineated*: believe, then *do*—repentance, humility, prayer for forgiveness, and (as the chapter develops) tangible charity.
- **Processual and retentive** — Distinguishes initial reception of remission (faith in the atonement) from ongoing retention “from day to day.” Not a completed transaction; maintained through humility, prayer, steadfastness, and ethical practice.
- **Soteriology integrated with social ethics** — Withholding substance from those in need disqualifies walking “guiltless before God” and retaining remission. “Are we not all beggars?” levels every person—king included—before God and one another.
- **Humble kingship and relational address** — Models humility about royal status while directing loyalty to the “Lord Omnipotent.” Inclusive lexicon (“My friends and my brethren, my kindred and my people”). Conditions presented as means of covenant relationship, not distant legalism. Scholarship often reads Mosiah 5 covenant-making as echoing ancient covenant-renewal patterns, with Benjamin in a suzerain-like role pointing beyond himself to the divine king.
- **Rhetorical and conditional framing** — Heavy “if…then” constructions and imperatives that turn belief into observable action. Salvation offered universally (“prepared…for all mankind”) yet conditional on response; phrasing redolent of covenantal stipulation language.

Collectively: salvation in Benjamin’s usage is covenantally structured, ethically embodied, communally oriented, and continually maintained—emphasized by speaker, temple/covenant setting, and rhetorical goals.

## Pre-exilic Hebrew constellation

To capture Benjamin’s blend of physical deliverance, covenantal adoption, absolute reliance on a redeemer, and communal obligation to the poor, look at a network of pre-exilic Biblical Hebrew terms—not a single abstract noun for “salvation” in the modern spiritual sense, but action-oriented, relational verbs and nouns (attested primarily in Iron Age II material: Torah, Deuteronomistic History, Psalms, Isaiah 1–39, etc.).

### 1. *Gaʾal* (גאל) — the kinsman-redeemer

- **Literal:** To redeem, act as a kinsman, buy back.
- **Context:** In ancient Israelite law, if a person fell into absolute poverty (a “beggar,” as Benjamin says) and sold themselves into slavery, the nearest capable relative—the *goʾel* (redeemer)—was obligated to pay the debt and restore freedom and family standing. Prominent in legal/covenantal contexts (e.g. Leviticus; Ruth precursors); relational loyalty parallels Benjamin’s ethical stipulations for retaining remission.
- **Benjamin:** Maps to royal-adoption theology. Humanity is in spiritual debt (“unprofitable servants”). Salvation is *familial* redemption: the people become “the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters” (Mosiah 5:7).

### 2. *Kaphar* (כפר) — the covering (atonement)

- **Literal:** To cover, pacify, make propitiation, ransom. Root of *Yom Kippur* (Day of Atonement).
- **Context:** In pre-600 BC temple theology, blood of a substitute sacrifice was smeared or sprinkled to “cover” sins and impurities, reconciling the people with a holy God.
- **Benjamin:** Salvation linked to the “atoning blood of Christ” (Mosiah 4:2). “Put off the natural man” aligns with *kaphar*—covering/removal of an impure, hostile state so one can stand before the Divine without being destroyed.

### 3. *Pada* (פדה) — the ransom price

- **Literal:** To ransom, deliver by paying a price.
- **Context:** Unlike *gaʾal* (family relationship), *pada* stresses the *economic* transaction of buying someone out of bondage or death.
- **Benjamin:** Heavy economic metaphors—even full service leaves one “unprofitable servants…indebted unto him” (Mosiah 2:21–24). Salvation as *pada*: ransom for debtors of absolute “nothingness” who cannot afford their own release.

### 4. *hesed* (חסד) — covenantal grace and loyalty

- **Literal:** Lovingkindness, steadfast love, covenant loyalty, grace. (President Nelson’s spelling: *hesed*; see [The Everlasting Covenant](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/liahona/2022/10/04-the-everlasting-covenant?lang=eng).)
- **Context:** Not merely emotion; action—unmerited, steadfast loyalty God shows covenant people, especially when they cannot help themselves.
- **Benjamin:** “Goodness of God, and his matchless power, and his wisdom, and his patience” (Mosiah 4:6) describes *hesed*. Salvation is accessible because God maintains His *hesed* toward people who are otherwise “dust of the earth.”

### 5. *Tzedaqah* (צדקה) — righteousness as communal justice

- **Literal:** Righteousness, justice; heavily associated with charity and doing right by the vulnerable.
- **Context:** Modern English “righteousness” often sounds like personal moral purity. In pre-exilic Hebrew, *tzedaqah* was highly social—you could not be righteous while ignoring the poor.
- **Benjamin:** Explains the jarring transition in Mosiah 4: to *retain* a remission of sins you must feed the hungry, clothe the naked, visit the sick (Mosiah 4:26). Salvation and *tzedaqah* are inseparable; receiving God’s *hesed* but refusing *tzedaqah* toward a fellow beggar voids covenantal rescue.

### 6. *Yasha* (ישע) — the broad space (and derivatives)

- **Literal:** To save, deliver, be spacious or wide.
- **Earliest physical concept:** Pulled from a tight, restricted, or besieged place into a broad, expansive, safe pasture. Active freedom from constriction or danger; used for physical rescue (enemies, peril) and divine deliverance in covenantal/moral contexts. Common in pre-exilic Psalms, early prophetic literature, historical books.
- **Derivatives:**
  - **יָשַׁע** (*yāšaʿ*, verb) — save, deliver, rescue, help, bring to safety.
  - **יְשׁוּעָה** (*yəšûʿâ*, noun) — salvation, deliverance, victory, help, welfare. God’s rescuing act for people who trust and remain faithful; covenantal settings stressing God’s power, wisdom, and response to humility/repentance. Roughly 77 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible, with strong pre-exilic attestation.
  - **יֵשַׁע** (*yēšaʿ*) — deliverance/salvation, often overlapping *yəšûʿâ*.
- **Benjamin:** Captures the relief and freedom the people experience—fall to the earth, joy and “peace of conscience” (Mosiah 4:3): from constricted guilt into wide, peaceful deliverance. Aligns with exclusive divine means, belief leading to action, and retention through faithfulness and charity (social deliverance mirroring divine *yeshuʿah*). In pre-exilic orations/psalms of covenant renewal, *yashaʿ* words often tie deliverance to remembering God’s acts, humility, and obedience—echoing retention “from day to day.”

### Other related pre-600 BC terms

- **פָּלַט** (*pālat*) — escape, deliver, bring to safety. Personal or communal escape from peril, sometimes with divine agency; early poetic and prophetic texts.
- **גָּאַל** (*gāʾal*) — see *gaʾal* above; included here as the same root in verb form.

**Strongest cluster for Mosiah 4:8:** *yāšaʿ* / *yəšûʿâ*—holistic deliverance, divinely initiated yet conditioned on faithfulness, encompassing rescue, victory, and sustained well-being. Semantic range is broader “rescue to well-being” than later specialized theological senses. Extra-biblical inscriptions offer limited abstract theological usage but confirm the root’s early presence in personal names and deliverance contexts.

## Modern translations of Mosiah 4:8

The Book of Mormon exists in over 110 languages. Official translations render the English core faithfully (“means whereby salvation cometh… none other salvation… conditions whereby man can be saved”) with no fundamental doctrinal alteration. Target-language vocabulary sometimes stresses *rescue from peril*, *liberation*, *wholeness/safety*, or *ongoing deliverance*—complementing English, which can feel more abstract or future-oriented.

### By language family

**Romance** (close cognates of Latin *salvatio*):

- **Spanish** — *salvación* / *ser salvo*. Active process (“being saved”).
- **French** — *salut* (also safety/well-being).
- **Portuguese / Italian** — *salvação* / *salvezza* (*salvezza* adds “safety”).

**Germanic:**

- **German** — *Errettung* / *errettet*: rescue from danger/peril—more immediate than abstract “salvation.”
- **Dutch** — *verlossing* or *heil*: liberation/redemption or wholeness.

**Slavic and other European:**

- **Russian** — *spasenie* (спасение): rescue/preservation; same root as practical emergency rescue (lifeguards, etc.).

**Asian:**

- **Japanese** — *sukui* (救い): rescue, help—deliverance from bondage or peril.
- **Korean** — *guwon* (구원): relief, rescue; regional character traditions often evoke untying a knot or pulling someone from fire—sin as entanglement the Savior unbinds.
- **Chinese** — *jiùshú* (救贖) or *jiùēn*: redemption/salvation grace—redemptive act and favor, close to atonement.

**Other samples:**

- **Indonesian/Malay** — *keselamatan* (root *selamat*: peaceful, safe, successful—cf. *selamat pagi*). Connects to “peace of conscience” Benjamin promises nearby.
- **Tagalog** — *kaligtasan* (root *ligtas*: safe, secure, out of harm’s way)—protective boundary.
- **Polynesian** (e.g. Samoan, Tongan) — often blend salvation with peace, wholeness, or divine protection.
- **Swahili** — *wokovu* (verb *okoa*: save, rescue, redeem)—practical rescue from danger, sickness, or enemies.

Non-Western languages frequently heighten *rescue from peril* or *restoration to wholeness*, enriching the conditional, retentive, and communal-ethical reading of the verse without contradicting doctrine. Modern translations prioritize fidelity while using natural target equivalents; stronger “rescue” fields (German, Japanese, Korean, etc.) make atonement-as-means and belief-to-action feel more immediate—aligned with earlier Hebrew/Greek breadth (rescue + well-being).

### Metaphor patterns across languages

When translators leave Western European defaults, word choice highlights different angles of Benjamin’s theology:

1. **Cleared debt (Middle Eastern)** — Benjamin’s economic imagery (“unprofitable servants,” Mosiah 2:22–24).
   - **Arabic** — *khalas* (خلاص): finished, cleared, free from a burden; everyday “it is done.” Salvation as settling an impossible spiritual debt.
   - **Persian (Farsi)** — *rastegāri* (رستگاری): deliverance/liberation from a confined or trapped state—mirrors Hebrew “wide place.”

2. **Safe harbor (Austronesian)** — protection from disaster, secure destination (*kaligtasan*, *keselamatan*—above).

3. **Upward pull (Indic)** — lifting from a lowly or burdened state.
   - **Hindi** — *uddhaar* (उद्धार): uplifting, pulling up from a degraded state. Fits “dust of the earth” and the Savior lifting the natural man.
   - **Tamil / Telugu** — often *Rakshana* (protection/guardianship) or *Mukti* (liberation from the cycle of suffering)—Christ as guardian who releases the soul.

4. **Survival and preservation (Slavic & African)** — same word as surviving war, famine, catastrophe (*spaseniye*, *wokovu*—above). Humanity in terminal danger without outside intervention.

5. **Release from knots (Sino-Tibetan region)** — *guwon* and related traditions (above): untying entanglement; sin as a complex knot.
